## Purpose

The product authenticates but has **no concept of who**. A survey of the shipped code confirms it
precisely: the Flask session carries a single flag, `rack_web_auth="1"`; the login endpoint accepts
`{"password": ...}` with no username; and the "user" it returns is `_auth_user()`, an environment
variable. Authentication today is a boolean, and configuration is standing in for identity.

Two consequences make this the blocking phase rather than a nice-to-have.

**The audit trail Phase 1 built cannot name anyone.** `audit/request_hook._principal()` reads
`session["rack_web_user"]` — a seam deliberately reserved for this phase — and that key is **written
nowhere in the codebase**. Every audit event therefore records `principal: "anonymous"`. Phase 1
delivered a trail whose most important field is a placeholder.

**Every operator attribution in the product is self-reported.** `operator_from_request()` reads the
`X-Rack-Operator` header, sanitises it, and is documented as *"advisory metadata only — never
trusted for authorization"*. It has **46 call sites** across 13 modules, and it is what stamps rack
lock claims and validation activity records. So the activity ledger that answers "what ran on this
rack, and who launched it" — the product's acceptance evidence — is advisory. Making that an
authenticated subject is what turns it into evidence.

Phase 3 (authorization) cannot begin without this: a policy engine needs a principal to evaluate.

This is a draft working artifact. It does not replace canonical SRD, STP, TAVA, nSpect or
release-gate evidence.

## Assumptions

1. **TLS is available and enforced** (Phase 0.5, REQ-30: TLS ≥ 1.2, self-signed by default). SRP is
   specified here *in addition to* TLS, not as a substitute for it.
2. Operators reach the dashboard through an SSH tunnel in normal operation, so the browser and the
   server are effectively same-origin and the TCP peer is loopback.
3. **Air-gapped sites are first-class** (REQ-37). Any new dependency must be installable offline, so
   new wheels are vendored into the image rather than fetched at deploy time.
4. The deployment holds a persistent `var/` directory. Identity data is deployment-local; there is no
   central identity service to defer to.
5. Operators are trusted staff (SRD assumption). This phase constrains *what they can do through the
   product* and makes their actions attributable; it does not defend against a hostile administrator
   with shell access to the host.
6. An OIDC identity provider is reachable **only at sites that have one**. Federation is therefore
   configurable and off by default, and its absence must not degrade local login.
7. The Phase 1 audit event's field set is fixed (FR-5). Phase 2 fills `principal` and `auth_method`
   with real values and adds no fields.

## Proposed Functional Requirements

| ID | Requirement | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| FR-1 | The product shall maintain a local identity store holding user records, group records and group membership, persisted in the deployment's own storage and surviving process and container restart. | user request; scope doc | Groups are in this phase deliberately: adding membership to a store that already holds credentials is a migration with an authentication outage in it |
| FR-2 | The **login identifier shall be the user's identity**: unique, **immutable once created**, and used unchanged as the principal in audit events and policy. No separate opaque identifier shall appear in a principal string or a policy document. | feature-lead direction 2026-08-25, replacing the uuid4 form | measured: `user:rkoike` is 11 bytes against `user:<uuid4>`'s 41, which is ~3 days of retention at FR-7b's ceiling — and an audit trail whose purpose is *who did what* is far more useful when it says `user:rkoike` than a uuid nobody can resolve while reading. **Immutability is the price, not a detail**: the uuid existed solely to survive a rename, so removing the uuid requires removing the rename |
| FR-2a | Every login identifier shall be unique across the identity store, compared **case-insensitively**, and shall be stored and rendered in a single canonical case. A create that would collide shall be refused. | feature-lead direction 2026-08-25 | two accounts differing only in case are indistinguishable to a human reading an audit event. Canonical case matters more now that the identifier *is* the principal: `user:Admin` and `user:admin` must not be two strings for one subject, or a policy matches one and misses the other |
| FR-2b | A login identifier shall **never be reused**. Deleting or disabling a user shall retire its identifier permanently, and a create shall be refused if the identifier has ever been used. | **consequence of FR-2, and the one that protects the audit trail** | this is the requirement that stops the trail becoming *actively wrong* rather than merely ambiguous. Without it: `jdoe` leaves, the account is deleted, a different `jdoe` is created — and every action the first person took is now attributed to the second. A policy naming `user:jdoe` would also transfer its grants to the new person, which is privilege transfer by clerical accident |
| FR-2c | A user record may carry a mutable display name for presentation. It shall not be an identifier, shall not appear in a principal string, and shall not be required to be unique. | consequence of FR-2's immutability | the honest answer to "someone's name changed": the login stays, the display name moves. Renaming the *identifier* is not supported, and a typo at creation is corrected by creating a new user and retiring the old identifier — which is what the audit trail should show anyway, since they are two principals |
| FR-3 | A local credential shall be persisted only as an SRP salt and verifier. No other representation of the password shall be written to the store. | user request ("secure, reliable and non reversible") | reworded from "no recoverable password material" — *recoverable* is a claim about cryptography that no test can settle. What a test **can** settle is which fields exist and that the plaintext appears in none of them |
| FR-4 | Local password authentication shall use the **SRP-6a** protocol, such that the plaintext password does not appear in any HTTP request body, header, query string or URL sent to the server. | user request; feature-lead decision 2026-08-25 (reaffirmed against the alternative of an argon2id verifier over TLS) | reworded from "never transmitted in any form": a *derived* value is a form of the password, so the original phrasing was unfalsifiable. Naming the four observation points makes it testable |
| FR-4c | The product shall reject any JWT whose header names an algorithm other than the one the deployment is configured to use, and shall never accept `alg: none`. | **gap found in self-review 2026-08-25** — the draft required tokens to be "signed" without constraining the algorithm | algorithm confusion is the classic JWT break: a token signed with `none`, or an RS256 verifier tricked into HS256 with the public key as the secret. Without this, FR-5's "signed" is satisfiable by an attacker |
| FR-4e | The SRP parameters shall be stated explicitly at every call site: hash **SHA-256 or stronger**, group **4096-bit or larger**, salt **at least 16 bytes**. The library's defaults shall not be relied upon. | **gap found in a dependency spike 2026-08-25**; group size raised by feature-lead direction 2026-08-25 | measured, not assumed: `srp` 1.0.22 defaults to `hash_alg=0`, which is **SHA-1**, and `salt_len=4`, a 32-bit salt. A correct-looking call to `create_salted_verification_key(user, pw)` therefore enrols a SHA-1 credential with a 4-byte salt. The parameters are configurable and verified working at SHA-256 / NG_4096 / 32-byte salt; the requirement exists because the *default* is the wrong one and nothing would fail if someone used it. **The floor is 4096, not 2048**: 2048-bit finite-field crypto is 112-bit security, which NIST SP 800-57 disallows after 2030 |
| FR-4f | The SRP exchange shall use the **RFC 5054 §2.6 padded** hash construction, pinned on whichever backend the `srp` package binds at import, and shall refuse to start if the pin cannot be verified. The group shall be the **RFC 5054 Appendix A 4096-bit** safe prime with `g = 5`, not a locally generated modulus. | **gap found during implementation 2026-08-25** | two findings. (1) The library defaults RFC padding **off**, computing a variant that interoperates only with itself; since the browser client is in-repo TypeScript and mainstream JS SRP libraries follow the RFC, a mismatch would fail *every* login while looking exactly like a wrong password. Measured: RFC↔RFC and legacy↔legacy authenticate, both cross pairs refuse. (2) The flag is **per-backend** — `srp/__init__.py` prefers the OpenSSL `_ctsrp` and falls back to `_pysrp`, each with its own `_rfc5054_compat` — so pinning the wrong module is a silent no-op. Which one binds is decided by `dlopen`, not by us: we get `_pysrp` on both macOS and `python:3.12-slim` only because `_ctsrp` wants `libssl.so` and the images ship `libssl.so.3`. Installing `libssl-dev` would flip it. Padding affects only `u` and `k`, not `gen_x`, so enabling it does **not** invalidate stored verifiers |
| FR-4g | The route that begins an SRP exchange shall be **rate limited per client**, and shall shed load rather than queue unbounded work, since it performs a 4096-bit modular exponentiation for an unauthenticated caller. | **consequence of the FR-4e group change, found by measuring it 2026-08-25** | `begin` costs ~52 ms of server CPU and must stay reachable unauthenticated — it *is* the login route. At one worker that is ~19 anonymous requests/second to saturate a core, against ~75 at 2048-bit, so raising the group made this ~4x worse. FR-4b makes it unavoidable rather than accidental: a cheap path for unknown logins would be an enumeration oracle, so *every* caller must get the expensive one. The bounded challenge table caps memory and does nothing for CPU. The limit belongs at the endpoint, which is the only layer that knows the caller |
| FR-4d | The product shall validate the issuer, audience and expiry of its **own** tokens, not only those from a federated provider. | **gap found in self-review 2026-08-25** — AC-14 covered the OIDC token's claims and nothing covered ours | a token minted for one deployment must not be accepted by another |
| FR-4a | The SRP implementation shall be a vendored dependency installable with no network access at deploy time. | REQ-37 air-gapped | a wheel in the image, not a `pip install` on the head node |
| FR-4b | A failed authentication shall not reveal whether the login name exists. | standard practice | the SRP exchange must proceed with a synthetic salt for an unknown user, at comparable cost |
| FR-5 | On successful authentication the product shall issue a **JWT id token** (who the subject is) and a **JWT access token** (what the session may do), signed by a deployment-held key. | user request | id/access split as specified |
| FR-5a | Browser-delivered tokens shall be transported in a cookie marked `HttpOnly`, `Secure` when TLS is configured, and `SameSite=Lax` or stricter — **not** in `localStorage` or `sessionStorage`. | design decision 2026-08-25, honouring "JWT everywhere" without an XSS token-theft path | script-readable storage means one XSS is one stolen session; the cookie keeps JWT semantics and removes that path |
| FR-5c | Every token shall carry its **type** (`id`, `access`, `refresh`), and verification shall require the caller to state the type it expects, refusing any other. | **gap found during implementation 2026-08-25** — FR-5 named the id/access split without requiring it be *enforced* | JWT libraries have no notion of token type, so nothing distinguishes our three tokens by default. The refresh token lives 48x longer than the access token, so presenting one as the other would authorise requests for half a day; the reverse would let an access token mint fresh ones forever. The expected type therefore has **no default parameter** — type confusion should need a wrong argument, not an omitted one |
| FR-6a | If no revocation check is registered, the product shall refuse **every** token rather than treat the absence as "nothing is revoked". | **design decision during implementation 2026-08-25** | fail-closed, because FR-6's invariant is *grants may be stale, denials may not*. Phase 1 supplies the precedent directly: the config materialise hook, when unregistered, produced wrong values on some clusters and an error on none, and needed `test_config_materialize_hook.py` purely to assert the wire existed. Here the same absence is a loud total refusal, caught by the first request rather than by an incident. Minting deliberately stays available so first-boot bootstrap (FR-10) can run before the revocation store is wired |
| FR-5b | Access tokens shall be short-lived and renewable without re-entering a password, and the renewal path shall itself be revocable. | FR-6 depends on it | short TTL is what bounds the staleness FR-6 permits |
| FR-6 | Before honouring any token-derived grant, the product shall consult a revocation record for the subject. A revocation shall take effect within **10 seconds**; a withdrawn *grant* may lag by up to the access-token lifetime. | scope doc; `docs/PHASE3_POLICY_ENGINE_SPIKE.md` §5b | "within seconds" was unquantified and therefore untestable. 10 s is the stated bound; it also caps any cache TTL on the revocation record. The invariant is *grants may be stale, denials may not* — a stateless JWT cannot be withdrawn before expiry, so this check is what makes JWT acceptable |
| FR-7 | The product shall support **OIDC federation** as an alternative authentication method, configurable per deployment and disabled by default, validating the provider's ID token before establishing a session. | user request; feature-lead decision 2026-08-25 (in Phase 2, not deferred) | absence of an IdP must not affect local login (assumption 6) |
| FR-7a | A federated subject shall be resolved to a local user record, so that groups, policy attachment and the audit trail work identically for local and federated principals. | design necessity | otherwise Phase 3 has two principal models |
| FR-8 | Groups shall be **flat**: a group shall contain users and shall not contain another group. | feature-lead decision 2026-08-25 | removes transitive resolution and the cycle guard it would need; revisit only with a stated need |
| FR-9 | Where a user-level and a group-level grant conflict, an explicit **user-level denial shall take precedence** over a group-level permission. | feature-lead decision 2026-08-25 | one sentence an operator can hold in their head; Phase 3's evaluator binds to it |
| FR-10 | On first boot with no existing user records, the product shall create a single administrative user with the login identifier **`admin`** and a **randomly generated password**, emit that password once through the deployment's own startup output, and store only its verifier. | user request; login name fixed by feature-lead direction 2026-08-25 | the password is never recoverable afterwards, by construction (FR-3) |
| FR-10b | The bootstrap in FR-10 shall run on **any** start that finds an empty identity store, including the first start after upgrading a deployment that previously authenticated by shared token or PAM. | consequence of FR-18; feature-lead direction 2026-08-25 | this is the requirement that prevents the upgrade from being a lockout: the legacy credential stops working in the same release that removes it, so the replacement must appear in the same start |
| FR-10a | The generated credential shall be marked as requiring a change on first use. | standard practice | a bootstrap credential that lives forever is a shared password |
| FR-11 | The **only** path to creating a user shall be an authenticated, authorised request. There shall be no self-registration and no unauthenticated provisioning endpoint. | user request | stated explicitly because its absence is the vulnerability |
| FR-12 | Authentication shall not be bypassable by any configuration input. No environment variable shall grant access, disable the gate, or carry a credential. No recovery code or credential-reset endpoint shall exist. Recovery from losing every administrative credential shall be by redeploying the identity store. | user request; strengthened by FR-18 | previously scoped to "a populated store", which left the empty-store case open. With the legacy methods removed there is no configuration path to authenticate at all, so the requirement is now unconditional |
| FR-13 | The product shall provide create, read, update, disable and delete operations for **users**, each as a distinctly named audited action. | user request; scope doc | split from the original combined requirement: one requirement covering three entities × four operations gave a single pass/fail for twelve claims |
| FR-13a | The product shall provide create, read, update and delete operations for **groups**, each as a distinctly named audited action. | as above | |
| FR-13b | The product shall provide add-member and remove-member operations for **group membership**, each as a distinctly named audited action. | as above | membership editing is the surface that makes groups worth having |
| FR-14 | The product shall support exporting the identity store in an **encrypted** form, and importing it, such that the export is useless without its key. | user request ("export the identity store in a secure way with key encryption") | for migration and backup; the export contains verifiers, not passwords |
| FR-15 | The audit event's `principal` shall be the authenticated subject's typed identifier in the form `user:<id>`, and `auth_method` shall name the mechanism used (`srp`, `oidc`), replacing the current constant `anonymous`/`none`. | Phase 1 FR-5; the reserved seam | fills `session["rack_web_user"]`, which is read today and never written. `token` and `pam` are **not** in the value set: FR-18 removes those mechanisms |
| FR-15a | Principal and resource identifiers shall be produced and parsed by a single module, and no other code shall construct or split them by string manipulation. | Decision 1a | the action vocabulary's lesson applied to identifiers: a grammar with one implementation, or three regexes that disagree |
| FR-16 | Operator attribution recorded against durable artifacts — rack lock claims and validation activities — shall be the authenticated principal, and shall not be derived from any client-supplied header. | the 46 `operator_from_request()` call sites | this is what turns the activity ledger into acceptance evidence. The trailing "a display name may remain" was dropped: *may* is not a requirement, and it made the clause untestable. Whether a display label survives is now open question 2 |
| FR-17 | The product shall provide operator surfaces for user administration and for group and membership administration, built on the existing frontend contexts and the Kaizen design system. | scope doc "admin UI"; REQ-44 | `UsersView`, `GroupsView` |
| FR-18 | The legacy authentication methods shall be **removed**, not deprecated. Specifically: the shared-token mode and its `RACK_WEB_AUTH_TOKEN`, the PAM mode and its `RACK_WEB_AUTH_MODE` / `RACK_WEB_AUTH_USER` / `RACK_WEB_PAM_SERVICE` / `RACK_WEB_PAM_HELPER_PYTHON`, and the vendored PAM bindings, shall no longer exist as authentication paths. Username-and-password against the identity store, or federation, shall be the only ways in. | feature-lead direction 2026-08-25 | **This reverses the previous FR-18, which required backward compatibility.** Recorded as a deliberate, accepted break of SRD REQ-41 — see *Breaking change* below. The live Nscale TX override currently carries `RACK_WEB_AUTH_TOKEN=nvis1234!`, a shared password in a committed deployment file, which is the concrete case for removal rather than deprecation |
| FR-18a | `RACK_WEB_AUTH_DISABLED` shall be **retained in this phase** as a development and test switch, and shall be unavailable once an identity store holds a user. | feature-lead decision 2026-08-25 | separated from FR-18 because this is a *switch*, not a credential. 67 test files and local demo development depend on it, so removing it in the same phase that replaces the credential model would make one change into two. Retained deliberately and with an expiry — FR-18b |
| FR-18b | The product shall provide a **test credential facility** that issues or retrieves a real credential for a named user, so that automated tests authenticate through the same path as an operator rather than bypassing the gate. Phase 3 shall not begin until this exists. | feature-lead direction 2026-08-25 | **The reason this is a requirement and not a preference:** authorization cannot be tested by a suite that never authenticates. Measured today: **2,286 of 2,339 tests reach every endpoint unauthenticated**, and the 53 that do exercise the gate use the shared-token mode FR-18 removes. Phase 3 has to assert that user A may do what user B may not, which needs two authenticated principals with different roles — unreachable from a bypass. It is also what makes auth a *first-class citizen* in the sense `core-designs.mdc` uses: a concern the product exercises, not one it switches off to get its own tests to pass. Recorded now, with a phase gate, because this programme's own scope doc opens by noting that two constructs were invisible rather than deferred |
| FR-18c | Once FR-18b exists, `RACK_WEB_AUTH_DISABLED` shall be removed, and the suite shall authenticate. | closes FR-18a | the expiry date on FR-18a, so "retained for now" does not become permanent by default |
| FR-19 | The login screen shall offer local username-and-password entry and, when federation is configured, a clearly labelled button per configured provider — initially **NVIDIA SSO**. | feature-lead direction 2026-08-25 | the button is the visible half of FR-7; NVIDIA SSO is the first provider, to be configured against Entra ID |
| FR-19a | A configured-but-unreachable provider shall leave local login usable, and shall say that the provider is unavailable rather than failing silently or blocking the form. | assumption 6; product's own absent-vs-zero rule | an IdP outage must not be an outage of the product |

## Acceptance Criteria

Every requirement above has at least one criterion.

| ID | Req | Criterion | Method | Verification idea |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AC-1 | FR-1 | A user, a group and a membership created through the API are all present after a process restart. | automated test | create, restart the app object, read back |
| AC-2 | FR-2 | An attempt to change a user's login identifier is refused, and the identifier appears unchanged in audit events written before and after any other edit to the record. | automated test | replaces the previous rename-survives criterion: rename is now the thing that must fail |
| AC-2a | FR-2b | Creating a user with the identifier of a previously deleted user is refused. | automated test | **the criterion that protects the trail.** Without it a recreated `jdoe` inherits the departed `jdoe`'s history and grants |
| AC-2b | FR-2b | After deletion, the identifier is still resolvable as retired — prior audit events naming it do not become unattributable. | automated test | retirement must not be the same as erasure, or the trail loses its subject |
| AC-2c | FR-2a | `Admin` and `admin` cannot both exist, and the principal string for either is the same canonical form. | automated test | asserts the canonicalisation, not just the refusal |
| AC-2d | FR-2c | Changing a display name does not change the principal in a subsequent audit event. | automated test | proves the display name is not load-bearing |
| AC-3 | FR-3 | No field of a persisted user record contains the password, and the record cannot be used to recover it. | automated test | create with a known password; assert it appears nowhere in the serialised store |
| AC-4 | FR-4 | Across a successful login, the plaintext password appears in no request body, header, query string or path reaching the server. | automated test | capture every request the client makes during login; assert the password string is absent from all four locations |
| AC-4a | FR-4c | A token with `alg: none` is refused, and a token signed with an algorithm other than the configured one is refused. | automated test | two negative cases; the second is the RS256→HS256 confusion attack |
| AC-4b | FR-4d | A token with a wrong issuer, a wrong audience, or past expiry is refused, for tokens the product minted itself. | automated test | three negative cases — the mirror of AC-14, which only covered federated tokens |
| AC-5c | FR-5c | Each of the six wrong (token type, expected type) pairs is refused, and `verify` cannot be called without naming an expected type. | automated test | all six, because the two dangerous directions are opposite: refresh-as-access buys 12 hours of authority, access-as-refresh buys perpetual renewal |
| AC-6b | FR-6 | A revoked subject, a revoked `jti`, and a token issued before a "log out everywhere" cutoff are each refused; a disabled or deleted login is refused even with no revocation row written; and revoking one token leaves the same user's other tokens working. | automated test | five separate claims because they are five distinct mechanisms, and the last is what makes FR-5b's revocable renewal usable rather than a blunt logout |
| AC-6c | FR-6 | Immediately after a "log out everywhere", existing tokens are refused **and a fresh login is accepted**; asserted over repeated cycles. | automated test | the regression guard for a real defect. A whole-second `iat` forced a cutoff a second into the future, which refused the user's own next login — so a password change looked like a broken account. Repeated cycles because a sub-second ordering bug presents as flakiness, not a failure |
| AC-6d | FR-6, NFR-3 | Repeated checks are served from cache; a revocation written by another process is still observed; the cache age ceiling is below FR-6's 10 s; and the check's p95 is within 5 ms. | automated test | the cache is the one component that can turn a denial into a grant silently. The "actually serving reads" clause is not redundant — it caught a read path that wrote, defeating its own cache |
| AC-6a | FR-6a | With no checker registered, verification of a validly signed, unexpired token is refused; the checker is never invoked for a token that failed signature or claim validation. | automated test | the second clause matters as much: a revocation checker handed attacker-controlled claims from an unverified token is a new attack surface, not a control |
| AC-4j | FR-4d | A token minted under one deployment secret is refused under another, and a rotated secret invalidates already-issued tokens without a restart. | automated test | pins what *actually* separates deployments — the derived key — since `iss` and `aud` are product constants and identical everywhere |
| AC-4h | FR-4e | The configured group is ≥ 4096 bits, its modulus equals RFC 5054 Appendix A's 4096-bit prime, its generator is 5, and `(N-1)/2` is prime. | automated test | the last clause is the safe-prime property, and it is the one a substituted or hand-generated modulus would fail silently. Costs ~2 s of Miller-Rabin and earns it |
| AC-4i | FR-4g | The SRP-begin route refuses a caller exceeding its configured rate, and the refusal does not perform the modular exponentiation. | automated test | shedding *before* the expensive work is the whole point; a limiter that rejects after computing has not removed the amplification |
| AC-4f | FR-4f | The active backend's RFC-5054 padding flag reads enabled; a deliberately mismatched exchange is refused; and switching the mode leaves an enrolled verifier byte-identical. | automated test | three separate claims: the pin holds, the pin matters, and the pin needed no credential migration |

## Decisions taken, and what they cost

Recorded because two reverse a recommendation, and the reasons should survive.

**SRP is kept, not replaced by an argon2id verifier over TLS.** The alternative was offered on the
grounds that Phase 0.5 already delivered enforced TLS, which covers SRP's headline benefit, and that
argon2id is one round trip, mainstream and easy to review. The decision is to keep SRP as originally
specified. What it buys: the password never exists server-side even transiently, so a terminating
proxy, a memory dump or a debug log cannot contain it. What it costs, and what this brief therefore
requires: a vendored wheel (FR-4a), a multi-round-trip login that the SPA must carry, and a
login-latency budget instead of a free one (NFR-2).

**JWTs are used for the browser as well as for API clients — but transported in an `HttpOnly`
cookie.** The specified design was "JWT everywhere". The risk in the naive reading is storing the
token where scripts can read it, which converts any XSS into a stolen session. FR-5a resolves that
without abandoning the decision: the token keeps its JWT semantics and the browser never exposes it
to script. This is why AC-9 asserts the negative — that no token reaches `localStorage` — rather
than only asserting the positive.

**A stateless token forces the revocation check to be a requirement, not a nicety.** FR-6 exists
because a JWT cannot be withdrawn before it expires. The invariant already settled for Phase 3 —
*grants may be stale, denials may not* — is what makes this acceptable, and AC-12 pins **both**
halves so a future optimisation cannot quietly trade the denial half away. NFR-3 borrows Phase 1's
5 ms budget because this check runs on every request, and Phase 1 learned the hard way that a
per-request store read costs 4.7 ms.

**OIDC is in Phase 2, and needs no new backend dependency.** Since PyJWT arrives for our own tokens,
an IdP's ID token can be validated with PyJWT plus a JWKS fetch, so `authlib` is not required. The
browser side can use `oidc-react` or MSAL. Air-gapped sites simply leave it disabled (assumption 6).

**Groups are flat and user-level denial wins.** Both chosen for reviewability: no transitive
resolution means no cycle guard, and the precedence rule fits in a sentence. Phase 3's evaluator
binds to FR-9, so changing it later is an evaluator change, not a schema change.

## Breaking change: the legacy authentication methods are removed

FR-18 reverses this brief's own earlier FR-18, which required backward compatibility. Recorded here
rather than buried in a table cell, because it is a deliberate, accepted break of **SRD REQ-41**
(backward-compatible identifiers and compatibility aliases) and it affects every deployment in
service.

**Why removal rather than deprecation.** A shared token is not a weaker identity, it is the absence
of one: it cannot name who acted, so the audit trail Phase 1 built stays anonymous for as long as the
token path exists. Keeping it alongside the identity store would mean the product has one door that
records who came through and another that does not — and the second door is the one an operator uses
when the first is inconvenient. The concrete case is already in the tree:
`deploy/nscale-tx.override.consolidated.yml` line 84 carries `RACK_WEB_AUTH_TOKEN=nvis1234!`, a
shared password committed to the repository.

**What breaks, specifically.** All four deployments — Mayo Clinic, Nscale TX, Firmus MEL01, Boston
Markley — authenticate by shared token today. On upgrade, the token stops working. **FR-10b is the
requirement that stops this being a lockout**: the same start that removes the legacy path
bootstraps `admin` with a generated password and prints it once. The operational sequence therefore
becomes: upgrade, read the password from the container's startup output, log in as `admin`, change
it, create real users. That sequence needs to be in the deployment documentation before this ships,
not after.

**The test suite is the larger mechanical cost.** 67 test files reference `RACK_WEB_AUTH*`, and
`backend/tests/conftest.py` sets `RACK_WEB_AUTH_DISABLED=1` suite-wide — which is why almost none of
the 2,339 tests exercise the gate today. FR-18a keeps the constraint (no production bypass) while
leaving the mechanism to design. The honest options, neither free:

- A session-injecting fixture, so tests authenticate as a real user instead of disabling
  authentication. Strictly better, because the suite would then exercise the gate rather than
  bypass it, and it is the larger change.
- A bypass confined to demonstration mode and unavailable once a store exists. Cheaper, and it keeps
  a bypass in the product.

**Two documents already contradict this and must be updated when it lands**: SRD **REQ-27** states
authentication supports "PAM locally and a shared token for container deployments", and
`docs/ENVIRONMENT.md` documents all five removed variables.

## Design Delta

Drafted 2026-08-25, after the requirements above. Decisions are recorded with what was rejected,
because the rejected option is usually the one someone proposes again in six months.

### Decision 1 — identity gets its own store, and this is not a preference

**Rejected: a `users` table in the catalog.** It was the cheap option and it looked right — the
catalog's `TinyDBRepo` already provides atomic writes, an `RLock` and an `fcntl` flock, which is
NFR-5 satisfied for free.

**It is disqualified by one fact.** `NVIS_CATALOG_FILE` selects *which catalog file* the process
reads: Firmus uses `var/store/catalog.json`, Boston uses `var/store/catalog-boston.json`. If users
lived in the catalog, **an environment variable would change who can log in** — a direct violation of
FR-12, which requires that no configuration input grants access. The catalog is also a document store
whose contents operators legitimately hand-edit and whose exports are plaintext configuration, and
neither property is acceptable for credential material.

There is a cleaner statement of the same point: the catalog answers *what this deployment can run and
where*. Identity answers *who may use it*. Those have different lifetimes, different blast radii and
different backup stories, and FR-14 wants an encrypted export of one and not the other.

**Chosen: a separate store that reuses the mechanism, not the file.** `_AtomicJSONStorage` is a
TinyDB `Storage` subclass parameterised only by path — verified: it has no coupling to catalog tables
— so identity gets its own file through the same class. One implementation of atomic-write-plus-lock,
two stores. Per `core-designs.mdc`, this is the documented exception: a genuinely new capability that
no existing citizen covers. **Identity is a new first-class citizen**, and the obligation that comes
with that is to add it to `core-designs.mdc` when it lands, so the next person inherits it.

Location: `var/identity/identity.json`, mode `0600`, under `/app/var` — which is the `rack-data`
named volume, so it survives a container recreate for the same reason the audit trail does (Phase 1,
TC-40).

### Decision 1a — principals are typed identifiers, rendered short, and resources use the same scheme

**Yes to a principal URN, with three qualifications that came out of measuring it.**

A bare `principal` string is what Phase 1 shipped, and it is already inadequate: `anonymous` says
nothing about *kind*. Once there are local users, federated users, groups and eventually service
principals, the field has to be self-describing or every consumer guesses. So:

```
principal  ::= "user:"    <login>     # local or federated — one namespace (FR-7a)
             | "group:"   <name>
             | "service:" <name>      # future: automation, not a person
             | "anonymous"            # retained: pre-auth and refused requests
resource   ::= <type> ":" <id> [ ":" <id> … ]    # type from the 36-type vocabulary
```

**The identifier is the login, not a uuid — revised 2026-08-25 after measuring it.** The first draft
used an opaque uuid4 on the usual reasoning that a rename must not orphan the trail. Measurement
changed the balance: `user:rkoike` is 11 bytes to `user:<uuid4>`'s 41, worth ~3 days of retention at
FR-7b's ceiling, and an audit trail exists to be *read* — `user:rkoike` answers "who did what" where a
uuid needs a lookup the reader may not have.

**The price is that two things become requirements, not conventions.** The uuid was doing exactly one
job, surviving a rename, so removing it means removing the rename (FR-2) and forbidding identifier
reuse (FR-2b). The second is the one that matters: without it, `jdoe` leaves, the account is deleted,
a different `jdoe` is created, and the trail now attributes the first person's actions to the second.
That is not ambiguity — it is an audit trail that is confidently wrong, about the product's own
acceptance evidence. A policy naming `user:jdoe` would transfer its grants too.

Which is why FR-2c exists: the human-facing name that *does* change is a display name, and it is
explicitly not an identifier.

**Qualification 1 — no `urn:` prefix, and the reason is measured rather than aesthetic.** A full
`urn:nvis:identity:user:<uuid>` is 59 bytes against `anonymous`'s 9, which is **+50 bytes on a
397-byte audit event, 12.6%**. `principal` appears in *every* event, so that is a ~12.6% cut in
retention days for the same byte ceiling — 41.8 days becomes roughly 37 at FR-7b's budget. The
`urn:nvis:identity:` prefix is constant across every principal in the system, so it carries **zero
information per event** while being the most-repeated bytes in the file. `user:<uuid>` keeps the type
discriminator, the single parse point and the extensibility, at 42 bytes. Registering an RFC 8141 NID
was never realistically on the table either.

**Qualification 2 — the pair is the storage form; the string is a rendering.** In memory and on disk a
principal is `(kind, id)`. The colon form is produced and consumed by **one module** with a
constructor and accessors — the same decision as the action vocabulary, and for the same reason:
structure encoded in a string gets parsed with a regex at the third call site and drifts from the
second. Nothing outside that module splits on `":"`.

**Qualification 3 — resources use the same scheme, or a policy language has two addressing models.**
`docs/RESOURCE_MODEL.md` already describes resources as `(type, id)`; the URN is their rendering, so
`rack.power:p5-r01` and `validation.activity:<uuid>` read the same way as `user:<uuid>`. This is what
lets a policy be written against principals and resources with one grammar.

**One asymmetry worth stating, because it falls out of Decision 1.** Resources are often
cluster-scoped — a Slurm job id is not unique across clusters — but **principals are not**. The
identity store is instance-wide precisely because it cannot live in the catalog, so a user means the
same thing whichever cluster is active, and a principal identifier needs no scope segment. That is a
consequence of the store decision rather than a separate choice.

**Rejected: Cedar's `Type::"id"` form.** It is the native syntax of one candidate engine and the
Phase 3 spike has not chosen an engine. Adopting one engine's serialisation before choosing the
engine would make the identifier a migration if the other wins. The typed pair feeds either.

### Decision 2 — the SRP exchange needs bounded server state, and that is the interesting part

SRP-6a is two round trips: the client sends a username **and its public value `A`** and gets back a
salt plus the server's public value `B`; it then sends a proof and gets the server's proof back.
Between those calls the server holds an ephemeral private value. That is **state in a stateless
API**, and it is where an SRP implementation usually goes wrong.

*(Corrected during implementation, 2026-08-25. This paragraph previously had `A` arriving in the
second leg, which would make the exchange three legs. The server cannot compute `B` without `A` —
`srp.Verifier` requires it at construction — so the first draft of `identity/srp.py` had a `begin()`
that could not work and was deleted rather than kept as a stub.)*

The design: a short-lived in-process challenge table keyed by an opaque id, bounded in size and
expiring in seconds, holding only the ephemeral values. Three properties it must have, each because
of a specific failure:

- **Bounded**, or an unauthenticated caller can exhaust memory by starting exchanges it never
  finishes. This is the same reasoning as Phase 1's flood guard and the lake sink's queue.
- **Single-use** — a challenge is consumed on verify, so a captured proof cannot be replayed.
- **Uniform on unknown users.** FR-4b requires that an unknown login be indistinguishable from a
  wrong password, so the init call must return a *synthetic* salt derived deterministically from the
  username and a server secret, at comparable cost. A real implementation that skips this leaks the
  user list through timing or through an obviously different response.

**Two things implementation added to the uniformity requirement**, both because "deterministic" and
"comparable cost" are easy to write and easy to miss:

*Deterministic needs a salt the library will not let you choose.*
`create_salted_verification_key` generates its own random salt and accepts none, so a decoy built
with it returns a **different salt on every probe** — which reveals non-existence by asking twice,
and differs across a restart where a real user's salt does not. `identity.srp.verifier_for_salt`
therefore computes `g ** gen_x(...) mod N` using the library's own `gen_x` and group with our salt.
That reaches into `srp._pysrp`, which is private API, so
`test_derived_verifier_matches_the_library` asserts it reproduces `create_salted_verification_key`
for the same salt — a library change then fails a test instead of silently breaking login. The salt
and the pseudo-password are HMACs keyed on the persisted `RACK_WEB_SECRET_KEY`, which is what makes
them stable across a restart.

*The group is RFC 5054's 4096-bit one, and the size is a dated decision rather than a default.*
2048-bit finite-field crypto is 112-bit security, which NIST SP 800-57 disallows after 2030, so the
floor is 4096 (FR-4e). Two things make the timing of this change the whole point. Changing the group
**invalidates every enrolled verifier** — `v = g**x mod N` depends on both, confirmed by computing
one credential under each group and seeing 255 bytes against 512 — so it is free today, with no
identity store existing or tracked anywhere, and a credential migration at any later date. And it
costs about **3.8x**: server CPU per login measured 31.7 ms → 121.6 ms. Irrelevant for a
once-per-session login at 20 operators, but it is the input to FR-4g below.

Note the generator is **5** at 4096 bits, where the 2048-bit group used 2. Worth stating because
"group 5" is easy to read as an IKE group number, and in that registry **group 5 is the 1536-bit
group** — weaker than the one being replaced. The 4096-bit modulus is IKE group 16.

*The 4096-bit group made `begin` a sharper CPU amplifier, which is why FR-4g exists.* `begin` must
stay reachable unauthenticated, and now costs ~52 ms of server CPU, so one worker saturates a core
at ~19 anonymous requests/second against ~75 before. FR-4b makes that unavoidable rather than
accidental: a cheap path for unknown logins would be an enumeration oracle, so every caller gets the
expensive one. The bounded challenge table caps memory and does nothing here — the answer is a rate
limit at the route, which is the only layer that knows the caller.

*Comparable cost is now measured, not intended.* `begin()` was timed at **15.35 ms for an unknown
login against 13.79 ms for a real one — 1.11x** (medians of 40). A test pins the ratio inside a wide
band, skipped under `CI` because a timing comparison on a shared runner is noise. The band is wide
deliberately: it catches an order-of-magnitude mistake such as an early return or a skipped modular
exponentiation, and does not pretend to be a constant-time proof.

**Thread-safe, not merely bounded.** The deployment runs `gunicorn --workers 1 --threads N
--worker-class gthread` (verified in the `Dockerfile` entrypoint), so there is one process and
several concurrent threads. The table therefore needs a lock, and the single-use consumption on
verify must be atomic — a check-then-delete without one is a replay window. The single worker is also
why design open question A is deferrable rather than urgent: a second worker would split the table
and break login, so if the worker count ever rises this becomes a correctness bug, not a slowdown.

That last point was implemented as a guard rather than the comment this paragraph originally called
for: `identity.srp.assert_single_worker_safe(workers)` raises with the reason. A comment does not
help the person who raised `--workers` for throughput and is now looking at a login that succeeds
about half the time — an intermittent failure that reads as a client bug and would cost hours.

### Decision 3 — JWT in an `HttpOnly` cookie, with the algorithm pinned

Tokens are minted HS256 over a key **derived** from the deployment secret rather than the secret
itself, so a JWT signing key leak is not a Flask session forgery key leak. Verification pins the
algorithm to exactly the configured one, which is what FR-4c requires: PyJWT will otherwise honour
the token's own `alg` header, and `alg: none` or an RS256→HS256 substitution both become valid
signatures.

The cookie replaces `session["rack_web_auth"]` as the source of the gate's verdict, and login writes
`session["rack_web_user"]` — the seam Phase 1 reserved and never filled. That single line is what
turns every audit event from `principal: anonymous` into a named subject (FR-15).

**Implementation found two silent holes in PyJWT 2.13's defaults**, both measured rather than
assumed, and neither of which raises or looks wrong at the call site:

- **`iss` is not validated at all** unless `issuer=` is passed. The claim can name anyone.
- **A token with no `exp` claim is accepted**, and never expires.

So verification passes `issuer=` and `audience=` *and* an explicit `require` list naming all seven
claims. `aud` is the one PyJWT does check unprompted, and pinning `algorithms=[...]` does correctly
refuse `alg: none` and an HS512→HS256 substitution — the pin works, it simply has to be written.

One framing correction worth keeping: **`iss` and `aud` do not separate deployments.** They are
product constants, identical on every install. The *derived key* is the boundary, since each
deployment has its own secret — verified by minting under one secret and failing verification under
another. Validating `iss`/`aud` buys something narrower and still worth having: it stops a token
minted for a different audience, most concretely a federated provider's ID token (also a JWT), being
presented as one of ours.

### Decision 4 — revocation is a cached read, sized by Phase 1's lesson

FR-6 puts a check on every authorised request, and NFR-3 gives it 5 ms at p95. Phase 1 established
exactly how that budget gets blown: `catalog_context.resolve()` read TinyDB on every audited request
and cost 4.74 ms, breaching the audit budget until it was cached on the store's generation counter.

So `revocation.py` uses the same pattern from the start: a generation counter bumped on every write,
and a cached read keyed on it. Not an optimisation to add later — the identical mistake is already
documented in this repository.

**Implementation found the generation counter is not sufficient on its own.** It is a *process-local*
integer, so a write from another process — an operator running a CLI against the same file — never
bumps it, and the cache would serve a stale **allow** indefinitely with no symptom, blowing FR-6's
10-second bound. The key therefore also includes the file's `stat()` identity (mtime and size, a few
microseconds), with a hard 5-second age ceiling as a backstop for a filesystem whose mtime
granularity hides a fast second write. Measured: **0.0034 ms cached, 0.092 ms uncached**, against the
5 ms budget.

**And a read path was quietly writing.** `revocation_snapshot()` originally pruned expired token rows
while reading, which was self-defeating twice: TinyDB's `remove()` rewrites the file *even when
nothing matches*, so every read became a write, and that write changed the mtime in the cache key —
so the cache was invalidated by its own reads and never hit once. A test asserting the cache actually
serves reads caught it. Expired rows are now filtered in memory and cleared by the next write.

**Four revocation granularities, because they answer different questions:** by subject (a user is
gone), by `jti` (one session or a leaked refresh token, without disturbing the user's others — which
is what makes FR-5b's revocable renewal path useful), by issue time (*log out everywhere*, and the
only mechanism available for it since outstanding token ids cannot be enumerated), and implicitly for
a login that is disabled or retired in the store — so deleting a user and forgetting the revocation
row does not leave their tokens live.

That third one carried a real bug worth recording, because the tempting fix is the wrong one. `iat`
at whole-second resolution cannot distinguish a token minted just before a cutoff from one just
after, so the first version pushed the cutoff a second into the future to stay safe — which then
refused the user's own *next* login for up to a second, making a password change look like a broken
account. Fixed by recording `iat` with sub-second precision (RFC 7519 §2 permits fractional
NumericDate), so the cutoff can be exactly *now*. Verified over 300 logout-then-relogin cycles: zero
old tokens accepted, zero fresh logins refused.

### Decision 5 — OIDC without a new backend dependency

PyJWT arrives for our own tokens, and an IdP's ID token is a JWT. So validation is PyJWT plus a
cached JWKS fetch, and `authlib` is not needed. The browser side can use `oidc-react` or MSAL.

The consequence to state: JWKS requires reaching the provider, so an air-gapped site simply leaves
federation unconfigured, and FR-19a makes an unreachable provider a visible message rather than a
broken login form.

### What this changes in existing citizens

- **Auth (citizen 11)** keeps its single gate; only the source of its verdict changes. `web_auth.py`
  loses `_token_verify`, `_pam_verify` and the mode selection.
- **The audit trail** gains real values in two fields it already carries. No schema change (FR-15).
- **Frontend contexts (citizen 12)**: `AuthContext` grows a username and the provider list;
  `LoginPage` grows a username field and federation buttons.
- **The action vocabulary** grows by the identity CRUD actions, each of which must be declared or the
  build fails (NFR-8) — so administering who may do what is itself audited.

### Design open questions

- **A. Multi-worker SRP state.** The challenge table is in-process. The deployment runs
  `--workers 1` today (verified), so this is deferrable — but it is a *correctness* dependency on a
  Dockerfile flag, not a performance one. Either share the table (Redis, or the store itself), pin a
  login to a worker, or assert `--workers 1` at startup so raising it fails loudly rather than
  producing intermittent login failures.
- **B. Token lifetimes.** FR-5b requires short-lived and renewable; the numbers are a deployment
  question (open question 3 above).
- **C. Where the JWT signing key lives.** Derived from `RACK_WEB_SECRET_KEY` is simplest and couples
  two concerns; its own file under `var/secrets/` is cleaner and is one more thing to bootstrap.
- **D. Does `operator_from_request()` survive as a display name?** FR-16 requires durable artifacts to
  carry the authenticated principal; whether a separate label remains, and what the 46 call sites do,
  is unresolved (open question 2 above).

## Explicit non-goals for this phase

- **Authorization.** No policy engine, no policy documents, no permission evaluation. The audit
  event's `decision` field stays `not-evaluated` and `policy_engine` stays `none`. That is Phase 3,
  and `PoliciesView` belongs there because a UI editing documents nothing evaluates invites false
  confidence.
- **Per-user cluster credentials.** Per-user SSH certificates and the removal of the `os.environ`
  materialisation are `docs/MULTI_USER_MULTI_CLUSTER_PLAN.md`, whose step 1 is blocked on this phase.
- **An audit trail viewer.** Recorded as a Phase 1 non-goal for a reason that still holds:
  authentication alone is insufficient, because without authorization every operator could read
  every other operator's actions.
- **Tamper evidence for the audit file** (threat T-51, candidate NFR-12) — still deferred.
- **Password policy and lockout.** Complexity rules, expiry and failed-attempt lockout are not
  required here; FR-10a covers only the bootstrap credential.
